## BellCounty Voting Machines

Voters will receive a blank ballot at check-in to insert into the ExpressVote ballot marking
device and make their selections. After reviewing their selections on the screen, voters will print
the ballot. Once voters have reviewed the selections printed on the ballot, they will insert the
ballot into the DS200 digital scanner for tabulation. This hybrid system allows for the
convenience and accuracy of electronic voting, as well as the security of an auditable paper
ballot that can be used for manual recounts.
ExpressVote Ballot Marking Device

The ExpressVote uses touch-screen technology that produces a paper-based ballot for tabulation.
As a marking device, the ExpressVote handles the entire marking process, eliminating marginal
marks and the need for interpretation of the voter's mark. Voters will receive an activation card
(ballot) with their ballot style printed on the card to begin the process. Voters will insert card into
the marking device, make their selections, verify the selections made, and print the ballot. Voters
then review the selections printed on the ballot for accuracy before taking the ballot over the
DS200 for tabulation.

An Information Paper by Dr. D. Roberts

Using paper ballots versus electronic voting equipment is a topic of ongoing discussion
among election experts and policymakers. Both options have advantages and considerations,
and opinions may vary depending on the context and specific concerns.
Election data means information that is created or managed in the operation of an election
system; Election system means a voting system and the technology used to support the conduct
of an election, including the election data processed or produced in the course of conducting an
election, such as voter registration information, ballot information, collected and tabulated
votes, election management processes and procedures, and other election-related documents
and election data (Texas Elections Code, Sec. 279.001). Electronic pollbooks are incredibly

The following Notice and remarks were issued and distributed to all stakeholders on
Texas Election Security by the Texas Secretary of State Elections Official Christina Worrell

compromised before the 2016 Election or in any subsequent elections. Additionally, The
Department of Homeland Security has offered a statement reiterating this fact as well.

forward‐thinking cybersecurity measures to further strengthen our state’s elections systems and

being cast or when they are being counted. (Section 129.054 Texas Election Code)
2. Only software certified by the Texas Secretary of State can be loaded on a computer used
for counting or accumulating vote totals. (Section 129.055, Texas Election Code)
3. Before and after use each day, all voting systems are sealed with locks and with seals
with unique serial numbers, and all election workers must follow the proper chain of
custody procedures during the election, including careful tracking of the serial numbers

The considerations include the factors associated with deciding on this compelling topic.
Existing Texas Elections laws outline the guidance, obligation, and authority of managing and
administering elections. The following are common findings:
Paper Ballots:
• Transparency: Paper ballots provide a tangible record of each voter’s choices, which
can be reviewed and audited manually to verify the accuracy of the results.
• Trust: Many people have confidence in paper-based systems because they are familiar
with them and believe they are less susceptible to tampering or hacking.
• Accessibility: Paper ballots can be more accessible to specific populations, such as the
elderly or visually impaired, as they can be marked in various ways to accommodate
different needs.
• Cost: Depending on the scale and complexity of the election, paper-based systems can be
more cost-effective, particularly in smaller jurisdictions with limited resources.

It’s important to note that election experts’ recommendations may vary based on factors such as
the specific technology used, security measures in place, potential vulnerabilities, and local
election laws. Conducting elections entirely by using paper ballots can have vulnerabilities,
including:
1. Human Error: Paper ballots can be susceptible to human errors during the voting,
counting, and tabulation processes. Mistakes can occur, such as misreading or
mishandling ballots or incorrectly recording and tallying votes.
2. Voter Mistakes: Paper ballots rely on voters correctly marking their choices, leading
to overvoting (marking more choices than allowed) or undervoting (not marking enough
choices), potentially resulting in invalidated or incomplete ballots.

3. Ballot Security: The physical security of paper ballots is crucial to prevent tampering

4. Vote Counting Efficiency: Counting paper ballots can be time-consuming, especially

and integrity of the process.
5. Voter Intimidation: In some situations, using paper ballots may make voters
susceptible to intimidation or coercion. Unlike electronic voting systems, which can
provide privacy and anonymity, paper ballots may expose voters to potential pressure or
influence from others.
6. Logistics and Storage: Conducting elections with paper ballots requires significant
logistical arrangements, including printing and distributing ballots, setting up polling
stations, and securely storing the paper ballots before and after the election. These
processes can be challenging and require additional resources and planning.
It’s worth noting that while paper ballots have vulnerabilities, they also possess certain
advantages, such as being resistant to certain types of cyberattacks and providing a physical
record that can be audited or recounted if necessary. An election’s overall security and integrity
depend on a combination of factors, including the voting system’s design, election officials’
training, and robust security protocols.
All digital systems are vulnerable to cyber-attacks, and voting systems are no exception.
The degree of vulnerability depends on various factors, including the design and
implementation of the voting systems, the security protocols in place, and the resources
dedicated to maintaining and improving these systems. Therefore, voting systems can be
vulnerable to several different types of attacks, including:
1. Hacking the voter registration databases: Attackers could delete or alter voter
registration information to cause confusion and disrupt the voting process.
2. Tampering with voting machines: Many voting machines are computers, and like any
other computer, they can potentially be hacked. This could allow an attacker to alter vote
counts or disrupt the voting process.
3. Disinformation campaigns: While not a direct attack on the voting systems
themselves, cyber attackers can use social media and other online platforms to spread
false information and influence public opinion.

Managing voting systems in the age of cyber vulnerability while improving election
integrity, transparency, and security is a complex challenge, but several strategies can be
employed:
1. Paper-based Systems: Despite the rise of digital technologies, paper-based voting
systems remain a reliable voting method. They provide a tangible, auditable trail and are
not vulnerable to cyber-attacks. These can be combined with electronic systems to ensure
a hard copy backup can be verified in case of discrepancies.
2. Secure Software: It’s crucial to use secure and tested software when using electronic
voting systems. Open-source software is a good option because independent entities can
audit it for potential vulnerabilities.

3. Regular Audits: Regular audits of the voting system are crucial to ensure the system

4. Multi-factor Authentication: Implementing multi-factor authentication can help
ensure that only eligible voters are allowed to vote. This is particularly important for
online voting systems with a higher risk of fraud.
5. Voter-Verified Paper Audit Trails (VVPAT): In electronic voting, VVPAT can be
used to allow voters to verify their vote was cast correctly, to create a record of votes
cast, and to provide an audit trail for verification of the result.
6. Encryption: Encryption should be used to protect data during transmission and at rest.
This includes both the votes themselves and the personal information of the voters.
7. Regular Patching and Updates: Like with any other software, voting systems need to
be updated with the latest patches and updates. This helps to protect them from known
vulnerabilities.
8. Education and Training: Election officials must be trained on the systems being
used, as well as on the potential threats and how to mitigate them. They should also be
aware of the procedures to follow in case of a suspected breach.
9. Independent Security Testing: Regular penetration tests and vulnerability
assessments by external, independent cybersecurity experts can help identify
vulnerabilities before they are exploited.
10. Transparency: The process should be designed transparently to allow voters to
understand how their votes are recorded, counted, and reported. This increases trust in the
system.
11. Contingency Planning: Have a well-prepared plan in case of a security breach or
system failure. This could include having backup systems, manual methods for recording
votes, and strategies for communicating with the public in the event of issues.
12. Voter education: Educate voters about the security measures in place and what they
should do to protect their information, like being aware of phishing attempts or
misinformation.
If the above list is implemented correctly and consistently, these measures can help
improve the integrity, transparency, and security of voting systems in the digital age.

Hash Validation Definition: Hash validation, as applied to voting systems, consists of the
process of verifying the integrity of the files and the source code of a specific voting system. A
hash is a mathematical function that creates a unique string of letters and numbers that
identifies a system and its programming. A specific hashing algorithm will always create the
exact string, allowing vendors and elections officials to compare hash values and confirm that
the voting system and its source code have not been altered.
During the hash validation process, a hash will be generated from the entity’s voting system and
compared to a trusted hash (golden file) that was generated by the U.S. Election Assistance
Commission (EAC) when the system was initially certified and tested at the federal level. Those
two hash values are compared to verify that the firmware and software used on the system
owned by the entity are the same as the firmware and software that was initially tested and
certified by the EAC and by the Secretary of State (SOS). If the generated hash is identical to
the trusted hash that was generated by the EAC, then the hash validation is successful.
General Procedures for Hash Validation
Four types of voting system testing shall be performed for each election within a jurisdiction.
The four tests are:
1. Hardware Diagnostic Test,
2. Logic and Accuracy (L&A) Test/Testing of Tabulation Equipment,
3. Hash Validation, and
4. Post-Election Audit (Partial Manual Count).

When considering a shift from electronic voting systems to paper ballots, several critical factors
should be taken into consideration:
1. Security: Electronic voting systems can be vulnerable to cybersecurity threats. Paper
ballots can eliminate this risk, but they are subject to different types of security threats,
such as physical tampering or destruction.
2. Accuracy: Paper ballots can be subject to human error in both marking and counting.
Electronic systems typically have features to prevent overvotes and can provide voters
with confirmation before casting, potentially reducing these errors.
3. Speed of Vote Counting: Electronic voting systems generally allow for faster vote
counting and results. Shifting to paper ballots could significantly slow down this process,
potentially delaying results.
4. Accessibility: Electronic voting systems can include features that make voting more
accessible to people with disabilities, such as screen readers for the visually impaired.
Paper ballots may not offer the same level of accessibility.
5. Cost: The cost of implementing and maintaining electronic voting systems can be
higher than paper-based systems, particularly considering the need for regular software
updates and hardware maintenance. However, paper ballots also involve costs for
printing, safe storage, and potentially manual counting.
6. Auditability: Paper ballots provide a physical record that can be reviewed in the case
of a recount or audit. While some electronic systems also have auditable paper trails, not
all do, which can be a significant concern.
7. Voter confidence: Regardless of the system’s actual security, if voters don’t trust it,
they might be less likely to vote. The perception of the voting system’s security and
integrity can have a significant impact on voter turnout.
8. Technical Support: Electronic voting requires a higher level of technical support and
expertise both for the maintenance of the systems and to assist voters who struggle with
the technology. With paper ballots, the technical support required is significantly less.
9. Environmental Impact: Paper ballots can have a larger environmental impact due to
the use of paper and the physical transportation of ballots compared to electronic systems.
10. Educating Voters: Any change in voting systems requires a campaign to educate
voters about how the new system works. This can involve significant time and cost.
Making such a decision should involve a comprehensive analysis and balancing of these factors,
considering the specific context of the county, its resources, its voters’ preferences and skills,
and the broader political and social environment. /---End of Paper---/ Dr. D. Roberts

Reasonable Impediment Declaration: If you do not possess one of the forms of acceptable photo identification listed above,
and cannot reasonably obtain such identification, you have the right to execute a Reasonable Impediment Declaration and
present a copy or original of one of the following supporting documents: (1) a government document that shows your name and
an address, including your voter registration certificate; (2) current utility bill; (3) bank statement; (4) government check; (5)
paycheck; or (6) (a) a certified domestic (from a U.S. state or territory) birth certificate or (b) a document confirming birth
admissible in a court of law which establishes your identity (which may include a foreign birth document).
The address on an acceptable photo identification or a supporting document does not have to match your address on the list of
registered voters.
Other Exemptions: Voters with a disability may apply with the county voter registrar for a permanent exemption to presenting
acceptable photo identification or following the Reasonable Impediment Declaration procedure in the county. Also, voters who
(1) have a consistent religious objections to being photographed or (2) do not present an acceptable form of photo identification
listed above or follow the Reasonable Impediment Declaration procedure because of certain natural disasters as declared by the
President of the United States or the Texas Governor, may vote a provisional ballot, appear at the voter registrar’s office within
six (6) calendar days after election day, and sign an affidavit swearing to the religious objection or natural disaster, in order for
a provisional ballot to be counted. Please contact your county voter registrar for more details.
The Secretary of State’s Office may be contacted toll-free at 1-800-252-VOTE (8683), via e-mail at
elections@sos.texas.gov, and by regular mail at: Elections Division, P.O. Box 12060, Austin, Texas 78711 to report
election violations.
