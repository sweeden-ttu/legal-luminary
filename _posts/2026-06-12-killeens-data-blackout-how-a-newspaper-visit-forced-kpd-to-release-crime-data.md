**Headline:** Killeen's Data Blackout: How a Newspaper Visit Forced KPD to Release Crime Data — And What They're Still Not Telling You

**Byline:** KDH News

**Date:** 2026-06-12

**Body:**

After more than 70 days of silence, the Killeen Police Department has agreed to resume releasing daily police blotter data to the Killeen Daily Herald — but only after a KDH reporter showed up at police headquarters in person.

The resolution, announced Tuesday, came after a months-long standoff that began March 30, when KPD abruptly stopped providing daily crime incident reports to the public, citing a "system upgrade." For over two months, residents of Killeen were left in the dark about crimes occurring in their neighborhoods.

**Transparency as a Two-Way Street**

Police Chief Pete Lopez, who agreed during Tuesday's meeting to email daily offense reports to KDH, described the technology transition as a routine migration between records management systems. "There was no way for the department to hide or withhold criminal activity logged by responding officers," Lopez said.

But transparency, as the chief himself noted, "is a two-way street." And the data flowing in one direction — from the public into private databases — tells a troubling story.

**What Data Is Public — and What Isn't**

A review of publicly available resources from the Killeen Police Department reveals a patchwork of information channels, some far more accessible to corporations than to ordinary citizens:

**Publicly Available KPD Data & RSS Feeds**

1. **KPD Press Releases (RSS Feed)** — `https://killeenpdnews.com/feed/` — WordPress-based site with press releases on crimes, crashes, arrests, and department activities. Updated regularly by PIO Ofelia Miramontez and Sergeant N. Holtzclaw.

2. **KPD on Social Media** — Facebook: `https://www.facebook.com/KilleenPD` / Twitter: `https://twitter.com/KilleenPD`

3. **Online Crime Map (LexisNexis Community Crime Map)** — `http://communitycrimemap.com` — Powered by LexisNexis. Not updated since late March 2026 — the same date the data blackout began.

4. **LexisNexis Accident Reports** — `https://policereports.lexisnexis.com` — Citizens can purchase accident reports through this portal. Data is processed and hosted by LexisNexis, not by the city.

5. **Department Transparency Page** — `https://www.killeentexas.gov/558/Department-Transparency` — Monthly crime statistics PDFs (2022-2026), Racial Profiling Reports, Use of Force Analysis, Pursuit Analysis, Internal Affairs reports, Agency Demographics, Drone/UAS reports.

6. **Annual Reports** — Archived PDF summaries.

7. **KPD Connect** — `https://kpdconnect.org` — Community engagement platform.

8. **Flock Safety Transparency** — Information on KPD's use of automated license plate reader technology.

9. **Sex Offender Registry** — Hosted on the city website.

10. **Internal Affairs Reports** — Archived annually.

11. **Traffic Stop Data** — Available on the city website.

**The LexisNexis Problem**

What the public does NOT have free access to is the raw data that KPD supplies to LexisNexis — a multinational data broker that collects and sells personal information on virtually every American.

LexisNexis Risk Solutions aggregates data from insurance claims, court records, motor vehicle reports, credit headers, and now — through its accident report and crime mapping platforms — police incident data from departments like Killeen. The same accident report that costs a Killeen resident a processing fee through the LexisNexis portal becomes part of a permanent data file sold to insurance companies, employers, and other data brokers.

Crime statistics should come from the courts, not from the police. When police control the narrative AND control the data pipeline to a private company like LexisNexis, there is no accountability.

**The Advertisement That Changed Everything**

The breakthrough came not through public records requests or city council inquiries, but through a paid advertisement in this newspaper. A weekend ad questioning city leadership on the missing data appears to have been the catalyst that brought KPD to the table — raising uncomfortable questions about who gets heard in Killeen city government.

**What Neighboring Cities Do Differently**

Copperas Cove, Harker Heights, and Lampasas maintain their own reporting methods for daily police calls. Killeen funnels its data through LexisNexis, creating a single point of failure and a commercial pipeline for public information.

**What's Still Missing**

- Will the daily blotter data be made available to the general public, or only to KDH News?
- How much are taxpayers paying for the LexisNexis contract?
- What data from Killeen residents has already been ingested into LexisNexis's broader data brokerage network?
- Who owns the "hundreds of thousands of calls with detailed information going back to 2015" that Chief Lopez described — the public, or the vendors who process it?

**Recommendation:** Killeen should follow the model of its neighbors — bring crime data reporting in-house, publish an open RSS feed of daily incident calls, and end the reliance on third-party data brokers who monetize public safety information.

As Chief Lopez said, transparency is a two-way street. Right now, the data flows out of Killeen into corporate databases — but the public is still waiting for it to flow back.
